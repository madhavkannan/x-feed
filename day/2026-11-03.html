The Anatomy of a Failed Stage 2 Uptrend

One of the most important lessons in Stan Weinstein's Stage Analysis is recognizing when a strong uptrend begins to deteriorate.

A Stage 2 uptrend doesn't usually turn into Stage 4 overnight. There are often warning signs along the way.

1. Loss of momentum: The stock stops making higher highs, rallies become weaker, and price begins forming lower highs.

2. Distribution: Down days occur on heavier volume while rallies attract weaker volume, suggesting selling pressure is increasing.

3. Moving average deterioration: Price repeatedly loses the 50-day SMA, and the 200-day SMA begins flattening after a sustained advance.

4. Failed breakouts: The stock breaks above resistance but quickly reverses, trapping recent buyers and creating overhead supply.

5. Breakdown: Price loses major support and eventually trades below a declining 200-day SMA, signaling a potential transition toward Stage 4.

Not every failed breakout means the uptrend is over. But when multiple warning signs appear together, the probability of a sustained trend reversal increases.

The key lesson: Technical deterioration often begins before the long-term trend officially changes. Recognizing that transition matters more than predicting the exact top.

Shrink Your Position Size, Not Your Process.

One of the biggest mistakes during market corrections is abandoning a proven framework or increasing position sizes to recover losses.

The approach is simple: adjust your exposure to market conditions, not your discipline.

1. Confirmed Uptrend: Normal position sizing when breakouts are working and market leadership is expanding.

2. Choppy Correction: Reduce positions to a quarter or half of normal size. Failed breakouts and increased volatility demand smaller exposure.

3. After a Follow-Through Day: Start small and gradually increase exposure only when your purchases begin working.

Here's the risk math:
- 10% position × 8% loss = 0.8% portfolio impact.
- 5% position × 8% loss = 0.4% portfolio impact.

Same percentage loss. Half the portfolio damage.

Reducing position size doesn't mean lowering your stock selection standards. Focus on relative strength, healthy price structures, and improving market breadth. If opportunities aren't there, staying in cash is also a valid decision.

Your entry rules, exit criteria, and stock selection process should remain consistent regardless of market conditions.

A follow-through day doesn't guarantee the correction is over. Let price action and market leadership confirm the improvement.

The market will offer new opportunities. Make sure you're in a position to take advantage of them.
