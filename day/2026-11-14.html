Want to know how to avoid major drawdowns and improve your returns in the stock market?

Understanding Stan Weinstein's Stage Analysis is a great place to start. Every stock moves through 4 stages!

One of the most important lessons I've learned from Stan Weinstein's Stage Analysis is understanding where a stock stands in its market cycle and when to buy, hold, reduce exposure, or stay away.

The framework primarily uses the 30-week moving average (roughly equivalent to the 150-day SMA), price action, volume, and relative strength.

Stage 1: Basing / Accumulation

What does it look like?
- Usually forms after a prolonged Stage 4 downtrend
- Stock stops making consistent lower lows and starts trading sideways
- 30-week moving average begins to flatten
- Price moves above and below the flattening moving average
- Selling pressure decreases, and volume often dries up

What does it mean?
- The stock is trying to establish a bottom, but buyers haven't taken full control yet. This is where I start building my watchlist and looking for improving technicals, higher lows, and signs of accumulation.

- Remember, a stock falling 50-70% doesn't automatically make it a Stage 1 base. It needs time to stabilize and build a proper structure.

Stage 2: Advancing/Uptrend

What does it look like?
- Stock breaks out above its Stage 1 base
- Breakout ideally happens on expanding volume
- 30-week moving average starts sloping upward
- Higher highs and higher lows
- Relative strength improves against the broader market

What does it mean?
- Buyers are taking control, and institutional accumulation may be supporting the uptrend.

- This is my favorite stage to own growth stocks! I generally look for Stage 1 → Stage 2 breakouts and constructive pullbacks to rising moving averages.
- The goal isn't to buy at the absolute bottom but to participate when the stock starts showing evidence of a sustainable uptrend.

Stage 3 vs. Stage 4 - This is where a lot of us make mistakes!

Stage 3: Topping / Distribution

What does it look like?
- Usually forms after a prolonged Stage 2 advance
- Momentum slows, and price starts moving sideways
- 30-week moving average begins flattening
- Price repeatedly crosses above and below the moving average
- Breakouts start failing, and selling volume may increase
- Major support levels are still holding

What does it mean?
- The previous uptrend is losing momentum, and the stock may be going through distribution.

- Stage 3 doesn't automatically mean a crash is coming. Some stocks consolidate and resume their uptrends.

- For me, this is where I become cautious, tighten risk management, consider trimming positions, and avoid aggressively adding exposure.

Stage 4: Declining / Downtrend

What does it look like?
- Stock breaks below major support or its Stage 3 range
- 30-week moving average starts sloping downward
- Price trades below the declining moving average
- Lower highs and lower lows
- Short-term rallies often fail near declining moving averages

What does it mean?
- Sellers are in control, and the stock is in a downtrend.

- One of the biggest lessons I've learned is that technicals often reveal weakness before fundamentals do.

- A company can continue reporting strong earnings while its stock is entering Stage 4 because the market is already pricing in future expectations.

The biggest difference between Stage 3 and Stage 4:

Stage 3 = Sideways price action, flattening moving averages, potential distribution.
Stage 4 = Support breakdown, declining moving averages, established downtrend.

Another important thing to understand is that Stage 1 and Stage 3 can look very similar!

Both involve sideways price action and flattening moving averages. The difference is what happened before:

- Sideways consolidation after a prolonged decline could be Stage 1.
