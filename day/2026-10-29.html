“I know what I should do, but I just can't do it.”

How many times have you said this?
Probably not more than me. 🤔 😄 Every trader experiences this.

Knowing what to do and actually doing it are two very different things.

@Andrew_Menaker explains why 👇
Andrew Menaker PhD @Andrew_Menaker · 29 Oct 2026, 00:08 SGT
The Deeper Reasons Why You Do What You Do:
(very long...do you have the patience to read it?)

In 1996 I sat with a bank teller a few hours after she’d had a gun pointed at her face. She wasn’t thinking about the robber anymore. She was thinking about her father, who used to raise his voice the same way the robber had.

Nothing about her rational, adult mind believed her father was in that bank. Her nervous system disagreed, and her nervous system doesn’t take votes from the rational mind. It just fires.

I spent years doing that kind of work, sitting with people in the minutes and days after something had overwhelmed their capacity to stay regulated. Many bank robberies, over a hundred of them for one institution alone.

A high profile incident at a naval airbase (also early in my career), where I had no time to consult theory and had to learn on the spot what happens to a human being when the ground disappears from under them.

Special forces operators, who taught me something I now consider the single most important sentence in performance psychology:

You do not rise to the level of your intentions, you fall to the level of your conditioning.

I didn’t walk into trading thinking any of that would apply. It applies completely.

A trader staring at an account that’s forty minutes underwater is not having a bad trading day. Often they are having a nervous system event, and the market on the screen is not the only market they’re trading.

There’s a second market running at the same time, built from every threat response, every old fear, every piece of conditioning they’ve ever absorbed.

I call it the Inner Market.

You don’t see the external market as it is. You see it as you are. And when those two markets disagree, the Inner Market usually wins, because it was installed first and it was installed harder.

In my 1:1 coaching work I've learned I\tt can be helpful to imagine two systems running inside you at all times.

One is conscious, slow, and can hold roughly one problem at a time. It’s the part of you narrating this sentence.

The other runs everything else, your heart rate, your balance, the tightness in your chest right before you hit sell a half second too late.

That second system doesn’t check in with you first. It’s efficient precisely because it skips that step, which is also exactly why it’s so hard to argue with once it’s running a pattern you no longer want.

This is why post -it notes, promises made to oneself, the great trading psychology book you just read......is forgotten or ignored so easily.

You do not rise to the level of your intentions, you fall to the level of your conditioning.

This is why willpower is such a poor tool for changing trading behavior.

You can decide with total sincerity, the night before, that tomorrow you will size correctly and wait for your setup. And you can still watch yourself chase a move by 9:42 the next morning.

That’s not weak character. That’s architecture. A small, effortful, easily depleted system trying to override a fast automatic one that’s had years of repetition behind it. It’s an unfair fight, and pretending it’s a fair one is where most discretionary traders lose the most money.

Where the Programming Actually Gets Written

Most of what’s driving that automatic system was installed early, often before you had the verbal capacity to argue with it. “I’m not enough.” “It’s dangerous to want something.” “If I get comfortable, it gets taken away.”

And staying with the familiar, even if it costs you, is the nervous system doing its job - new territory gets flagged as unfamiliar, and it carries a unique unease under the surface...this is why so many traders give back the profits made over the past 3 weeks.

Back to the early devlopment:
A child doesn’t get to reason through a painful, new, or unfamiliar experience and file it correctly. There’s no internal editor yet capable of saying, wait, is this actually true?

So it gets absorbed as fact, and it gets built into the architecture of a brain that is, at that age, unusually plastic and unusually literal.

That matters for you as a trader because the belief didn’t arrive with a label on it that says “childhood material, does not apply to markets.”

It just shows up as the flinch before you take profit too early, or the freeze right when a position needs managing, or the certainty that you personally are the kind of person who blows up good accounts.

The nervous system doesn’t file these things by category. It files them by intensity.

Neurons That Fire Together, Wire Together

This is a long post...and if you got this far...if there's one key idea to keep from all of this, keep this one. It’s called Hebbian learning, and it’s about as solid as anything gets in neuroscience.

When two neurons fire together repeatedly, the connection between them physically strengthens.

And that cuts in your favor as much as against you. If a pattern got built through repetition, it can be rebuilt through repetition.

There’s no rare gift required and no secret shortcut that skips the reps.

The brain keeps reorganizing itself across the entire lifespan. Neuroscientists Antonio Damasio and Lisa Feldman Barrett have both, from different angles, made the case that the brain isn’t a passive recorder of the world at all. It’s a prediction machine, constantly forecasting what should happen next based on what it already believes, then filtering incoming reality through that forecast.

Which means two traders can watch the same price action and have two completely different nervous systems tell them two completely different stories about what it means and what to do.

The Gap Between the Event and the Reaction

There’s a hard line between two kinds of memory. Explicit memory is what you’d testify to, the facts, the dates, the words someone used. Implicit memory is procedural and emotional, mostly out of reach of direct recall, which is why a setup that merely resembles an old losing trade can spike your heart rate with no conscious memory of the original loss attached to it at all.

That’s the mismatch behind a sentence I hear constantly in this work:

“I know what I should do, but just can't do it"

You’re not being irrational. You’re running an implicit prediction your conscious mind never got invited to review.

Real change starts in the half second between the trigger and the automatic reaction. Widen that gap even slightly, notice the trigger, notice the thought, the bodily sensation, the feeling, and you get leverage over a pattern that used to run you completely.

Skip that gap and you get the same result you’ve always gotten.

This is also part of reason for keeping a written trading journal that goes past P&L, one that tracks the internal state around the trade, not just the entries and exits.

Externalizing a thought onto paper drags implicit material into a system that’s actually capable of evaluating it.

That’s not a soft, optional add-on to trading psychology. It’s the mechanism.

Identity Beats Strategy, Every Time
And It's why most attempts at fixing trading behavior don’t work.

A trader tries to bolt a new rule onto an old identity.

Three weeks of discipline, then a snap back to the exact old pattern. If somewhere underneath the strategy you still believe “I’m not a disciplined trader,” or "I am not enough", the nervous system will keep steering you back toward evidence that confirms it, because confirming existing beliefs is close to the entire job of a predictive brain.

Change the underlying belief about who you are as a trader, and the behavior stops being a daily fight you have to win. It starts matching who you’ve actually decided you are.

State Comes Before Insight

The brain is not equally open to new input at every moment. A dysregulated nervous system, flooded, hypervigilant, defended, is not in a state where new learning lands.

This is precisely what I saw over and over in early in my career....crisis work, in the bank robbery debriefs, the military threat assessment work, in the sessions with people who’d just been through something that overwhelmed their capacity to stay regulated.

You cannot install a new belief into a system that’s actively defending itself. States of genuine calm, focused attention, reduced defensiveness, make the mind receptive in a way a flooded, threat state simply can’t match.

Emotion is what encodes a belief, not repetition on its own. A belief absorbed during real fear, shame, or humiliation gets burned in fast, sometimes in a single exposure, because emotional arousal amplifies memory consolidation.

This is why one brutal drawdown can outweigh months of clean, boring, profitable trading in a trader’s actual sense of who they are. And it’s why a new belief has to be paired with real felt conviction to have any chance of competing with the old one.

Flat repetition doesn’t stand a chance against a belief that arrived soaked in feeling.

It's also why affirmations don't work so well for traders.

Evidence Does the Work Affirmation Can’t

Telling yourself “I’m a disciplined trader” while your last six months of trade logs say otherwise doesn’t just fail to help. It can backfire, because the nervous system checks new statements against the evidence bank it’s already built and flags anything that doesn’t match.

What actually works - what I see in my coaching practice and it shows up consistently in the clinical literature - is building an evidence case. Naming the belief you want installed, then going back through your own trading history and finding the specific, concrete moments that already support it.

Ten real instances where you did hold the position, did size correctly, did wait for your setup, do more rewiring work than a hundred repetitions of a sentence you don’t yet believe.

This Takes Longer Than You Want It To

Structurally embedded change takes at least several weeks (sometimes longer) of consistent practice, not a weekend and not a single insight, however good that insight felt in the moment.

That’s frustrating to hear if you’re carrying a pattern that’s many years old and hoping this week is the one where it finally resolves. But it was built through thousands of small repeated moments spread across years, and undoing it runs on the same physical rules.

The genuinely useful part of that fact is that the timeline is knowable. It isn’t an indefinite wait for a feeling to fade on its own. It’s a process with a method and a reasonably predictable arc, provided you actually do the work instead of just understanding it.

This is where skilled 1:1 coaching comes in.

You’ve spent hundreds of hours training your read on price. Almost nobody spend seven a fraction of that time training the nervous system that is actually reading the price.
