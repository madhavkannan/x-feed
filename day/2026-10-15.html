Gartner says 80% of neocloud providers will stop operating independently by 2030 and I agree.

Sorry but not all of them will be winners, most of them will disappear even if demand is sky high.

If you read my longer takes you know that I think that as a category, neoclouds aren't a great business.

Capex drags on you every quarter, and the only way out is escape velocity: growing faster than your own spending.

Meaning most will be acquired, merged or starved of capital.

In June Gartner forecasted neoclouds taking 20% of the AI cloud market by 2030. Fewer providers plus a bigger share means the survivors get much, much larger.

Consolidation is how the winners get made.

So the real question is which one escapes, and here's why I think $NBIS is built differently:

+Customers fund the build. Prepayments covered 50–60% of the capex on its Q2 deals. Most neoclouds borrow against their GPUs, while Nebius gets its customers to pay up front.

+ The margins are already there: about 77% gross margin and a 41% adjusted EBITDA margin in Q2, on revenue up 454% year over year.

+ Pricing keeps rising. GPU prices went up another 17–21% on October 1, and new contracts are signing at $20–25M per MW.

+ It isn't drowning in interest. Last quarter $CRWV paid $640M of interest against $128M of adjusted operating income. Nebius's debt is mostly low-coupon convertibles.

+ The power is locked in. Contracted power has been raised three times this year, to 5 GW.

Gartner's real point is that the industry as a whole won't earn a 12% ROIC until 2032. An industry average tells you how the typical provider does, and I'm not betting on the typical provider. $NBIS is built different.

The risk with Nebius is financing, because the 5 GW build still needs about $250B.

So far they've done a stellar job handling it.

Macro comes and goes, but great companies endure.

I was thinking about what would actually make me sell my NBIS shares.

Simplified, these are the five things that keep me honest, with how healthy each one looks today (1 = bad / 5= good):

+ Financing gets too expensive (3/5)
 NBIS still has to raise roughly $250B to build out its 5 GW, and only about 20% is secured. If converts and credit get much more expensive, or the market stops taking their paper, growth slows or the share count balloons.

+ Dilution outruns growth (4/5)
 Some dilution is the price of the build-out, and I'm fine with that. What would break the thesis is ARR per share stalling while the share count keeps climbing.

+ Pricing rolls over (5/5)
 The bull case leans on new contracts at $20–25M per MW and GPU prices that are still rising. If contract pricing or on-demand GPU prices start falling, the economics behind every gigawatt shrink.

+ Customers stop paying up front (4/5)
 Prepayments covering 50–60% of capex tell me customers can't find this capacity elsewhere. If those shrink, demand is cooling.

+ Power slips (3/5)
 Contracted power that doesn't come online on time is revenue that doesn't show up, and power is the slowest input in the whole stack.

Demand is the easy part right now.

The weakest scores are on financing and delivery, and that's where I'll be watching.

if you're wondering why the 10-year is down today even though CPI had a horrible print...which should be causing yields to go up because that means we have a higher chance of a rate hike due to more inflation...

i *think* it's because the bond market wants to see the stock market go down.

and not just stocks, but AI/semi/capex stocks in particular.

the theory i've had over the past couple of weeks is that as we see more issuance of corporate debt in order to fuel capex...that is obviously competing with the long end of the curve for US treasuries

i mean, one can even argue that bond yields we're going to go up regardless of the oil issues because those yields simply couldn't stay at 4% if meta/google/amzn/msft were offering 6-7% on their corporate debt

so the obvious answer now could be for there to be LESS competition with the US 10-year...but the only way for that to happen is for their to be lower-yielding debt from the hyperscalers that are issuing bonds to fuel their capex spend

well...the only way that happens is if the capex stops, slows down, or the cost to build these datacenters drop so that the borrowing costs drop

which is why we have seen yields RISE every day over the past few weeks when semis go up and we are seeing them go down when semis go down...it's like the credit market is saying "we will only buy bonds if you guys stop buying semis"

this doesn't mean that stocks writ-large have to go down, but it does mean that the credit markets are asking for something to break in the equity markets and its like they want semis to be the main culprit. if semis keep going to the moon, cool, but then so will yields and then maybe a 6% 10-year is what breaks everything?

not sure if this theory is right but the objectively true part is the competition between US corp debt and treasuries, if all that corp debt is going to buy semis then when semis go down, it makes sense for treasuries to go up

the easiest way to get SOME relief here is for Trump to end the war so gas/energy comes down and puts relief on inflation...but today's CPI showed the opposite and yields STILL went down because semis got hit

it's a very, very weird time to understand how the bond market is navigating the AI trade
